The idea

I wanted something that would learn the market with me.

Not a signal service, and not a bot that promises returns. Something that would read the same things a careful person reads, make a call, and then be judged on it — publicly, over months, without anyone quietly correcting it.

Where it started

I have wanted to build some kind of intelligence around trading for a long time. Not to get rich from it — to understand it. The market is one of the few places where you can be confidently, measurably wrong, and I wanted something that would be wrong in the open so I could see how.

Then I found TradingAgents, a framework where several agents argue a stock out between them: a market analyst, a news analyst, a sentiment analyst, a fundamentals analyst, a bull and a bear who debate, then a trader and a risk team who decide. That was the shape I had been looking for. It was not a black box giving me a number. It was a group of readers I could listen to.

So I started building an app around it, on the hardware already sitting in my house.

What it became

The first version helped me. It analysed a watchlist I chose, and I decided what to do about it. That turned out to be the flaw. Every result was a mix of the model's judgement and mine, and afterwards there was no way to separate them. If the book went up, I could not tell you who was responsible.

So I took myself out of it. Every manual control went: all twenty-three Discord commands, every button that could add a ticker, start an analysis or place a trade. The agent got an allowance of $10,000, a bill of $0.05 for every analysis it orders, and no help at all.

It started on 2 September 2026, with an empty book, a freshly reset brokerage account and nothing on its watchlist.

That is the experiment. Not "can an AI make money" — that question is unanswerable at this sample size and anyone claiming otherwise is selling something. The question is narrower and more interesting: given real data, a real cost for information, and no human to lean on, what does a small model actually do?

The hardware

It runs at home, on cards nobody would choose for this.

  • Seven AMD RX 6600s, 8 GiB each, one analysis per card
  • 32 GB of host RAM, which turned out to be the thing that mattered
  • ROCm with an override, because these chips are not officially supported
  • About 700 W across the pool while a sweep is running

Measured properly on 2 September: fourteen analyses at once take 42.8 minutes, and the CPU is the bottleneck, not the GPUs. The cards sit around half busy while the host CPU is pinned at 100%, because this model family keeps its per-layer embeddings in host RAM. An eighth card would change nothing.

What I tried, and what did not work

This is the half worth reading, and the half most projects leave out.

Five models that confidently made things up

Small models are fast, and several of them cannot drive a tool-calling loop at all. They do not fail loudly. They answer, fluently, having invented the data. llama3.2:3b reported "no available market data for AAPL" and then issued a Buy anyway. phi4-mini printed its tool call as text and made up 2023 prices for a stock trading at $308.

The tell was not the writing, which was fine. It was the token count: they spent 42–45k prompt tokens against a working model's 103k, because they never read the data there was to reason over.

lfm2.5:8b broke that rule and taught me a better one. It is the fastest model that has ever fit this hardware, it read 77–96k tokens, and it still invented every price — from different years in different runs, which rules out a stale cache and leaves recall from training. Its model card claims tool calling as a strength. Both things are true and neither predicted anything here.

The cloud, which was faster and is still not what runs

Gemini Flash-Lite does an analysis in 1.2 to 1.6 minutes against 7 to 10 locally. A nine-ticker sweep is eleven minutes of wall clock instead of eighty-five of GPU. It is not close.

Two things stopped it. The whole history to date would have cost about $7.49 at list against $0.26 of marginal electricity — but that comparison only works because the server would be powered anyway, so it is not the honest reason. The honest reason is the $10-a-month cap on the account, which a daily full sweep exceeds. Speed was the argument for moving, and cost was never it.

Choosing a model that is twice as slow, on purpose

The model that runs is gemma4-e4b-qat-128k — a custom build of Gemma 4's 4B effective model at its full 131,072-token context, entirely on the GPU in 3.7 GiB.

It is about twice as slow as the 2B model it replaced, and I switched to it anyway. The smaller model would not use the candidate menu: shown a list of stocks it could pay to research, it chose to research nothing in two runs out of four, and answered "no research" three real mornings running. The bigger one picked four or five names with a stated reason each, in four runs out of four.

Speed rules a model out. Behaviour rules it in. This agent's whole job is choosing what to look at, and the faster model would not do the job.

The thing that still bothers me

The same ticker, on the same day, minutes apart, returns different decisions. Measured on 2 September across fourteen names run twice: two of twelve agreed. Three flipped from bearish to bullish outright.

That is temperature 1, which is Gemma's own recommended setting, and it is not a defect. But it means one analysis is one sample, and it puts a floor under what any prompt change can be credited with. A change that moves one morning's decision has moved nothing you could detect.

Why it is all published

Every decision the agent makes is here, with the prompt it was given and the answer it returned, word for word. So is every analysis, every graded outcome, and every change I have made to it, dated and with the reason.

That last part is the one I would ask anyone building something like this to copy. Behaviour is mostly prompt. A month of runs across three prompt revisions is three experiments with no way to tell them apart, unless somebody wrote down when the question changed. So the rule here is that a change goes in the journal before it goes in the code.

It is a small experiment on cheap hardware with a small model and a small sample. I would rather it be honest about that than impressive.
