Method

How this is run, and what it cannot tell you. Written for anyone deciding whether the numbers on this site are worth believing.

The question

Not "can an AI make money". That question cannot be answered at this sample size, in one market regime, with one model, and anyone claiming to have answered it is selling something.

The question here is narrower: given real market data, a real cost for information, and no human to lean on, what does a small language model actually do? Does it choose sensibly what to study? Does it size positions it can afford? Does it change its mind for reasons that survive reading?

The setup

  • One agent, one simulated brokerage account, one book.
  • A fixed budget, and it is told what that is. The broker's real balance is never shown — the sandbox account holds far more, and if that number reached the prompt the budget would mean nothing.
  • $0.05 per analysis, charged every morning for every ticker it keeps on its watchlist, out of the same money it trades with.
  • No human input. No control on this site or in Discord can add a ticker, start an analysis or place a trade.

What is controlled, and what is not

Held constant: the model, the prompt, the analyst pipeline, the debate rounds, the research price and the budget. Any change to these is dated in the journal before it is made.

Not controlled, and not controllable: the market. Every result on this site comes from one stretch of one market's history. A strategy that looks good in a rising market has told you almost nothing about itself.

How a call is graded

Each analysis carries a horizon — 14 days for a swing call — and is judged when that arrives, three ways:

  • Absolute. A Buy passes if the price rose. A Sell passes if it fell. A Hold passes if the price stayed inside a band.
  • Against SPY. A Buy must have beaten the index over the same window; a Sell must have lagged it.
  • Against its own target. Whether the price the analysis named was reached at any point in the window.

The second is the one that counts. An absolute win rate of 70% in a rising market can be worse than useless — holding the index and doing nothing would have beaten it.

What this design cannot tell you

This is the section to read before any number on this site.

One analysis is one sample

The model runs at temperature 1, which is its own publisher's recommended setting. Measured on 2 September 2026, fourteen tickers were analysed twice, minutes apart, on the same prices: two of twelve comparable analyses reached the same decision. Three flipped from bearish to bullish outright.

That is not a defect and lowering the temperature is not the fix — an earlier attempt to do exactly that treated a symptom of a smaller model's capability as a sampling problem, and was reverted. But it means no single signal is evidence, and it puts a floor under what any change to the prompt can be credited with. A change that moves one morning's decision has moved nothing detectable.

The sample is small and the regime is one

A few hundred graded signals over a few months, in whatever the market happened to be doing. Twenty resolved calls is the point at which a win rate stops being noise; it is nowhere near the point at which it becomes a finding about markets.

Simulated fills are not real fills

Orders go to a broker's sandbox. Fills there are cleaner than they would be with real money: no partial fills to speak of, no market impact, and a spread that behaves. A strategy trading small, liquid names is least affected by this, which is what the agent does — but "least affected" is not "unaffected".

Survivorship in the candidate list

The names the agent may choose from come from a screener of liquid, actively traded stocks. That list is drawn today, from companies that still exist and still trade. It is not the list it would have seen at the start of the window.

The analyses are not independent of each other

Every analysis on a given morning reads the same macro data and much of the same news. A day when the model is wrong about the market tends to be a day it is wrong about several stocks at once, so the effective sample is smaller than the count of signals suggests.

A known intermittent fault

The market analyst sometimes prints its tool call as text instead of making it, or answers without fetching anything. It is caught and retried, and the retry usually works. It predates every fix made for it, and it has not been eliminated.

What runs it

  • Model: gemma4-e4b-qat-128k — a 4B-effective build at its full 131,072-token context, entirely on one GPU in 3.7 GiB.
  • Hardware: seven AMD RX 6600s, 8 GiB each, one analysis per card, 32 GB of host RAM.
  • Cost per analysis: about 19 LLM calls and 133,000 tokens, roughly 19 minutes at seven concurrent.
  • Data: Webull for quotes and the paper account, yfinance for price history, FRED for macro series, Reddit's public feeds for sentiment.

Measured on 2 September: the CPU is the bottleneck, not the GPUs. The cards sit around 60% busy while the host CPU is pinned, because this model family keeps its per-layer embeddings in host RAM. Running fourteen analyses at once instead of seven takes the same wall clock and doubles the latency of each, so seven is what runs.

Why every prompt is published

Behaviour here is mostly prompt. A month of runs across three prompt revisions is three experiments with no way to tell them apart, unless someone wrote down when the question changed.

So the rule is that a change goes in the journal — dated, with the reason — before it goes in the code. And every decision pass keeps the prompt it was given and the answer it returned, word for word, on the Decisions page.

Reproducing it

The code is open. The documentation covers deploying it yourself, including running without a GPU pool by pointing it at a hosted model instead. The API is read-only and unauthenticated on the published copy, so the raw data behind every page on this site can be fetched directly.
